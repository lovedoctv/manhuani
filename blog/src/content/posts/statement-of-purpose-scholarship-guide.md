Your statement of purpose is often the only place a scholarship committee hears your voice. Grades and references show what you've done. The statement shows where you're going, and why giving you the money makes sense.

Most applications from Africa are turned down for the same few reasons: the statement is generic, it tells a long life story, or it never says what the applicant will do after the course. This guide shows you how to avoid all three.

## What the committee wants to know

Every good statement answers five questions:

1. **What do you want to study?** Name the course, not just the field.
2. **Why this course and this university?** Mention modules, research groups or teachers that match your plans.
3. **What have you done so far?** Your degree, work, projects and results, with numbers where you can.
4. **What will you do after?** A specific job, project or change you'll make at home.
5. **Why you?** What makes you more likely than other applicants to use this chance well.

If a paragraph doesn't answer one of these, cut it.

## A structure that works

**Opening (one short paragraph).** Start with the problem you want to solve, not your childhood. For example: "In my three years as a nurse in Kano, I saw mothers travel 60 km for a scan that could be done in their village."

**Your background (two paragraphs).** What you studied, what you've done at work, and what you achieved. Use evidence: "I trained 14 health workers" beats "I am passionate about health".

**Why this course (one or two paragraphs).** Link the course to your plan. Name two or three modules, a professor's research or a practical part of the course, and say how each one helps.

**Your plan after the course (one paragraph).** Be specific about the role, the organisation or the project, and what will change because of it. Scholarships funded by governments and foundations especially want to see that you'll use the skills at home.

**Closing (two or three sentences).** Bring it back to the problem from your opening, and say in one line why you're ready.

## Mistakes that sink applications

- **One statement for every application.** Committees can tell. Change the course, university and plan for each one.
- **Starting with "Since I was a child".** It wastes your best lines. Start with the problem or your work.
- **Lists of qualities.** "Hardworking, passionate and dedicated" says nothing. Show it with an example.
- **No plan after the course.** This is the most common reason strong candidates lose.
- **Copying templates or letting AI write it.** Reviewers read thousands of statements, and copied or machine-written text sounds the same. Use tools to check grammar, but the story and the plan must be yours.
- **Ignoring the word limit.** Going over can get you rejected before anyone reads it.

## Scholarship essays are different

Some scholarships ask several short questions instead of one statement, for example about leadership, networking or your career plan. Answer each question directly, and use a different example in each answer. The same five questions above still apply, just spread across the answers.

## Before you submit: a checklist

- It names the exact course and university.
- Every paragraph answers one of the five questions.
- There's at least one number or result in your background section.
- Your plan after the course is specific enough that someone could check it in five years.
- Someone who knows your field has read it, and someone outside your field understood it.
- It's within the word limit, with no spelling mistakes.
